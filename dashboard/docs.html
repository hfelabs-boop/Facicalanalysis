How each metric is calculated

Every number on the console comes from the steps below. Constants are the defaults in CogSenseConfig (cogsense/config.py). The browser/phone engine (dashboard/engine.js) is a port of the Python engine and gives the same results; a parity test checks this frame by frame.

Pipeline overview

  1. Landmarks. MediaPipe Face Landmarker returns 478 face-mesh points per frame, plus 52 learned blendshape scores.
  2. Normalize. The mesh is rotated into a face-anchored frame and scaled by inter-ocular distance, which removes head pose and camera distance.
  3. Features. 13 geometric measurements are taken from the normalized mesh (brow heights, eye openness, lip-corner depth, and so on).
  4. Baseline-relative evidence. Each feature is compared with the operator’s own neutral baseline and mapped to a 0–1 value per AU.
  5. Fusion & conditioning. Geometric evidence is blended with blendshape evidence; blinks and speech are masked; values are smoothed.
  6. Cognitive metrics. AUs, blink rate, gaze and posture feed MES, CFI and the event detectors.

Units & normalization

Landmarks arrive as (x/W, y/H, z/W). They are converted to pixels, then expressed in a face frame:

  • x axis runs from the subject’s right eye to the left eye (average of the outer corners, inner corners and the face sides).
  • y axis runs from forehead to chin, made perpendicular to x.
  • z axis is the cross product (pointing away from the camera).
  • Origin is the nose bridge (landmark 168).
  • Scale is the inter-ocular distance (IOD): the distance between the two eye centres.

All distances below are in IOD units. A brow shift of 0.08 means 8 % of the eye-to-eye distance, whatever the face size or camera distance. Head pose comes from the same frame:

yaw = atan2(R₀₂, R₂₂) pitch = asin(−R₁₂) roll = atan2(R₁₀, R₁₁)

Because every feature is measured in this frame, rotating the head within ±30° yaw, ±20° pitch or ±20° roll does not change the features (the tests check this).

Baseline calibration

AUs measure change from this person’s neutral face, not absolute shape. That absorbs resting brow height, natural asymmetry and habitual squint.

Action units

Each AU starts as a clipped 0–1 geometric evidence value. Let Δfeature = current − baseline, and clip(x) limit x to 0…1. The divisors are the scales in config: the displacement that counts as full intensity.

AU4 — Brow lowerer

Primary measure of mental workload and strain.

innerDrop = −(Δbrow_inner_h_right + Δbrow_inner_h_left) / 2 gapDrop = −Δbrow_gap AU4 = clip( 0.6 · innerDrop / 0.08 + 0.4 · gapDrop / 0.10 )

brow_inner_h is the height of the inner brow above the eye-corner line; brow_gap is the horizontal distance between the two inner brows. Lowering the brows and drawing them together both raise AU4.

AU1 — Inner brow raiser

AU1 = clip( −innerDrop / 0.08 ) (the inner brow rising above baseline)

AU2 — Outer brow raiser

outerRise = (Δbrow_outer_h_right + Δbrow_outer_h_left) / 2 AU2 = clip( outerRise / 0.09 )

AU1 and AU2 are reported separately; the automation-surprise detector uses their average.

AU7 — Lid tightener

Visual-search effort, fine discrimination, glare.

EAR = (|p2−p6| + |p3−p5|) / (2·|p1−p4|) eye aspect ratio, averaged over both eyes earDrop = 1 − EAR / EAR_baseline lidRise = −(Δlower_lid_right + Δlower_lid_left) / 2 lower lid moving up AU7 = clip( 0.5 · earDrop / 0.42 + 0.5 · lidRise / 0.05 )

While a blink is in progress the eye aperture collapses, so AU7 is held at its pre-blink value rather than reported as lid tightening.

AU14 — Dimpler

Frustration or annoyance (tightened lip corners).

AU14 = clip( 0.6 · Δlip_corner_depth / 0.06 + 0.4 · Δmouth_width / 0.06 )

lip_corner_depth is how far the mouth corners sit behind the centre of the lips. This is the weakest signal from a single camera, so it benefits most from blendshape fusion and from a trained model (see limits).

AU45 / AU43 — Blinks and eye closure

ratio = EAR / EAR_baseline closed when ratio < 0.45; reopens when ratio > 0.60 (hysteresis stops flicker)

Blendshape fusion

MediaPipe also outputs learned blendshape scores. Each is made baseline-relative, then averaged with the geometric evidence:

rel = clip( (score − resting) / (1 − resting) ) AU = 0.5 · geometric + 0.5 · blendshape

If a blendshape is missing for a frame, that AU uses geometry alone.

Smoothing

Each AU passes through an exponential moving average with a time constant of 0.04 s, adjusted for the actual time between frames:

α = 1 − exp(−Δt / 0.04) value ← value + α · (target − value)

This removes landmark jitter but still follows sub-500 ms transients.

Signal conditioning

Speech & chewing mask

Talking and chewing move the lower face rhythmically. The detector watches two signals over a 0.6 s window: inner-lip aperture and chin-to-nose distance.

std = max( stdev(aperture), stdev(jaw_drop) ) active = std > 0.018 OR ( mouth-open amount > 0.07 AND std > 0.009 ) stays active for 0.4 s after the last trigger

When active, speech_interference_detected is true, AU14 is multiplied by 0.3 and confidence by 0.85. Speech frames are also excluded from calibration.

Cognitive metrics

Mental Effort Score (MES, 0–100)

The PRD names the inputs (sustained AU4 and blink-rate suppression) but leaves the formula blank, so this is the implemented definition.

S = clip( 1 − blink_rate / resting_blink_rate ) blink-rate suppression e = 0.7 · AU4 + 0.3 · S per-frame effort MES = 100 · mean(e over the last 5 s)

Research evidence for this formula →

If no blink rate is available yet (first 5 s), e = AU4. The resting blink rate comes from calibration (or 17/min).

Worked example. AU4 held at 0.60 for 5 s, blinking 8/min against a resting 17/min: S = 1 − 8/17 = 0.529, e = 0.7·0.60 + 0.3·0.529 = 0.579, so MES = 57.9. With no blink data the same face scores 60.0.

A person who stops blinking entirely adds up to 30 points even with a neutral brow. Blink suppression goes with visual demand, but blinking rises with non-visual mental load and fatigue, so this term is contested; see the evidence.

Cognitive Friction Index (CFI, 0–1)

A continuous index that rises when a brow-lowering spike coincides with annoyance or erratic eye movement.

rise = AU4 − (lowest AU4 in the last 200 ms) C = max( clip(AU14 / 0.30), clip(saccade_rate / 3 Hz) ) co-occurrence CFI = clip( AU4 · (0.6 + 0.4 · C) + 0.15 · clip(rise / 0.25) ) CFI is halved if a task completion was logged in the last 2 s

Research evidence for this formula →

So AU4 alone gives at most 0.6 of its value; AU14 or fast saccades lift that to the full AU4, and a sharp rise adds up to 0.15.

Worked example. Steady AU4 = 0.80, AU14 = 0.15 (C = 0.5), no recent rise: CFI = 0.80 · (0.6 + 0.4·0.5) = 0.64. If the operator had just completed a task (within 2 s): 0.32.

The console and report mark moments where CFI exceeds 0.70.

CFI event (discrete)

A CFI_EVENT is logged when all hold:

  1. AU4 spike: AU4 > 0.65 and it rose by ≥ 0.25 within 200 ms.
  2. Co-occurrence (within 300 ms of the spike): AU14 ≥ 0.30 or saccade rate ≥ 3 per second.
  3. No task completion in the previous 2 s (the effort was productive, not friction).
  4. At least 1 s since the previous CFI event.

The event records the peak CFI, which trigger fired (AU14 or SACCADES) and the active AOI.

Automation surprise

brow raise: (AU1 + AU2) / 2 > 0.50 surge: AU4 ≥ 0.50 and rose ≥ 0.20 within 200 ms event: a surge within 800 ms after a brow raise

It logs AUTOMATION_SURPRISE once and holds automation_surprise_flag for 1 s. The pattern is a startle or novelty response followed by effortful re-evaluation, which can mark a human-autonomy hand-off breakdown.

Mental Effort Score: formula and evidence

MES = 100 · mean over 5 s of ( 0.7 · AU4 + 0.3 · S ), S = clip( 1 − blink_rate / resting_blink_rate )
TermEvidenceCaveat
AU4
(weight 0.7)		 Facial EMG over the corrugator supercilii, the muscle behind brow lowering, rises with mental effort. Topolinski & Strack (2015) review this, citing van Boxtel & Jessurun (1993), Waterink & van Boxtel (1994) and others, and note the frontalis responds to a comparable degree.

On video, Grafsgaard et al. (2013) coded 67 students in computer-mediated tutoring with an automatic AU tool: average AU4 intensity in the first five minutes correlated with end-of-session self-reported frustration (r = 0.41, p = 0.001). They read AU4 as possibly reflecting effortful thought or confusion that may turn into frustration. In AutoTutor studies by D’Mello and colleagues, summarised in the same paper, AU4 and AU7 correlated with confusion.		 Corrugator activity also tracks negative affect, so AU4 alone cannot separate effort from displeasure. The video result is a session-level correlation in one task, not a moment-by-moment measure.
Baseline-relative AUs Grafsgaard et al. found raw automatic AU output “varied significantly across individuals” and adjusted it “by subtracting the baseline (average) output value by facial action unit for each student”, the same idea as the 15 s calibration. Their baseline was the student’s session average, ours is a neutral-face calibration. Not directly compared.
Blink-rate suppression
(weight 0.3)		 Benedetto et al. (2011) summarise Recarte et al. (2008): blink rate is affected by both mental workload and visual demand, “which act in opposition to each other, the former leading to BR increase, the latter to BR decrease”. They also report, citing Stern et al. (1994), that blink rate increases with time on task (fatigue), and that blink duration shortens as visual workload rises (citing Veltman & Gaillard 1996; Ahlstrom & Friedman-Berg 2006). Suppression fits visually demanding displays such as C2 consoles. It does not capture non-visual mental load or fatigue, where blinking increases. In our formula an increase is clipped to S = 0, so it adds nothing rather than subtracting. Benedetto et al. also stress large between-person variability in blink rate.
0.7 / 0.3 weights, 5 s windowNone found.Engineering choices. Config: mes_au4_weight, mes_window_s. Setting mes_au4_weight to 1.0 removes the blink term.

Cognitive Friction Index: formula and evidence

CFI = clip( AU4 · (0.6 + 0.4 · C) + 0.15 · clip(rise / 0.25) ), C = max( clip(AU14 / 0.30), clip(saccade_rate / 3 Hz) ) halved for 2 s after a task completion
TermEvidenceCaveat
AU4 level and spike AU4 ↔ frustration, r = 0.41 (Grafsgaard et al., above). The “sharp rise” term (≥ 0.25 in 200 ms) and the 0.65 spike threshold have no direct source. Frustration is inferred from session averages in the literature; the spike rule assumes moments of friction show up as fast AU4 onsets. In an induced-frustration driving study (Ihme et al., 2018, N = 28) AU4 occurred about equally in frustrated and calm minutes (36.4 vs 34.6; r = .17, not significant), and the authors suggest it may reflect the concentration driving requires. AU4 therefore fits effort better than friction.
AU14 In the same study average AU14 intensity in the first five minutes correlated with frustration (r = 0.32, p = 0.010). In Ihme et al.’s driving study the AU14 difference was not significant (9.1 vs 7.8; p = .07, r = .20). The authors also found AU14 frequency in the last five minutes correlated with learning gain (r = 0.52), describe it as frequent “mouth fidgeting”, note that expert judges in earlier work did not identify AU14 as a frustration indicator, and suggest it can coincide with a thoughtful state. AU14 is therefore a weak, ambiguous marker on its own.
Saccade rate Goldberg & Kotval (1999) proposed eye-movement measures for interface evaluation: the number of saccades indicates the amount of search activity, and a longer scanpath can indicate less efficient search, for example from a poor layout. That supports scanning behaviour as a usability cue, not a fixed 3-per-second cut-off. Rayner (1998) reports fixations of about 200–300 ms between saccades (mean 275 ms in visual search, 330 ms in scene perception, 225 ms in silent reading), which works out to roughly 3 saccades per second in ordinary viewing: 2.7 to 3.9 depending on the task, from 1000 ms ÷ (mean fixation + a 30–50 ms saccade). See gaps.
Co-occurrence of channels Kapoor, Burleson & Picard (2007): with 24 participants using a learning companion, a model using multiple non-verbal channels predicted frustration at 79 % accuracy (chance 58 %). Shows that combining cues works moderately and that no single facial cue is enough. It does not validate our specific rule.
Task-completion gate
and AOI context		 NASA-TLX treats effort and frustration as separate workload dimensions (Hart & Staveland, 1988). The gate encodes the same distinction: strain while the task progresses is effort, strain with no progress is friction. Design logic, not an empirical result. The 2 s window and ×0.5 factor are untested.

Automation surprise: evidence

The concept of automation surprise, an operator caught off guard by what automation does, comes from Sarter, Woods & Billings (1997). Whether it shows on the face is a separate question.

  • Reisenzein et al. (2006), eight experiments in which surprise was induced by disconfirming an expectation: surprise expressions appeared in only 4–25 % of participants although self-reports and behaviour showed surprise in most, most displays were brow raising alone, and the full three-component display was never seen. EMG did not find notably more brow raising.
  • So AU1+2 is the most likely visible component, but its absence says little. The detector will miss most surprises and is best read as a high-precision, low-recall flag.
  • The AU4 surge that follows is consistent with Topolinski & Strack (2015), who report corrugator (brow-lowering) activity as an index of immediate negative affect in surprise.

Where the evidence disagrees with the defaults

  1. Blink suppression is only right for visual load. It under-reads non-visual mental load and fatigue (blink rate rises). The blink-duration shortening reported for visual load is better supported than blink rate and is not used yet. Option: lower the blink weight, or add blink duration.
  2. The 3 saccades/s trigger is at the normal viewing rate. The SACCADES route to a CFI event can fire during ordinary scanning. Option: compare with the operator’s own baseline rate during the task instead of a fixed number. The AU14 route is unaffected. Note that the built-in simulator uses about 1.25 saccades/s as “normal”, so its results do not expose this.
  3. AU14 is ambiguous. It tracks frustration and also concentration or fidgeting. The co-occurrence with an AU4 spike adds some specificity, but this is not tested.
  4. AU4 does not separate friction from concentration. In Ihme et al. (2018) it occurred equally in frustrated and calm minutes, and the lip tightener and presser (AU23/24) were the AUs that differed. CFI leans on AU4 for up to 0.6 of its value. Option: require a lower-face or contextual cue before CFI rises. See other facial signals.
  5. Surprise recall is low by nature. Treat AUTOMATION_SURPRISE events as confirmed cases, not a count of all surprises.

A general limit on interpretation

Barrett et al. (2019) reviewed the evidence on inferring emotion from facial movements and found that how people express emotions varies substantially across cultures, situations and even people within a single situation, and identified significant challenges to reliably inferring emotion from facial movements alone. They propose reliability, specificity, generalizability and validity as the tests an inference must pass. For this reason MES and CFI should be read as behavioural cues relative to the same person and session, together with context (AOI, task events, posture), not as readings of an inner state.

How to validate against ground truth

NASA-TLX is the standard criterion: Hart & Staveland (1988) defined six subscales including mental demand, effort and frustration, and Hart (2006) reviewed 550 studies using it. Not yet done here. A minimal protocol:

  1. Record each trial with cogsense run --record, with conditions that differ in difficulty (a manipulation check) and a known-friction UI variant.
  2. Collect NASA-TLX subscales after each trial.
  3. Compare, within person, mean MES vs Mental Demand and Effort and CFI events per minute and mean CFI vs Frustration (rank correlation), and check that MES and CFI differ between the easy and hard conditions.
  4. Refit the weights and thresholds (CogSenseConfig) on part of the data and report results on held-out participants.

First, a reality check on AU detection accuracy

  • Which AUs the benchmark datasets label. BP4D labels AU1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 10, 12, 14, 15, 17, 23 and 24. DISFA labels 12 AUs (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9, 12, 15, 17, 20, 25, 26) and is usually scored on 8 of them. So AU7 and AU14 appear only in BP4D, and AU45 (blink) in neither, which means blink detection needs its own validation, for example against manually coded blinks.
  • What published detectors reach. In the comparison table of Jin et al. (2024), nine deep-learning methods score (frame-based F1, subject-exclusive 3-fold) AU4 43.0–61.0, AU7 67.0–80.8, AU14 54.1–69.4, AU1 36.4–64.5, AU2 35.2–57.4, AU23 31.7–50.9 and AU24 30.0–58.2 on BP4D, and AU4 37.4–80.2 on DISFA. No method in that table reaches 0.82 on any of AU1, AU2, AU4, AU7, AU14, AU23 or AU24.
  • F1 is not like-for-like. Hinduja et al. (2024) found F1-binary correlates strongly with how often an AU occurs (0.907 on BP4D, 0.76 on BP4D+, 0.843 on DISFA), so scores differ by AU and dataset partly because of prevalence.
  • Implication. The PRD’s F1 ≥ 0.82 acceptance target is above every published number for these AUs, and this module’s geometric mapping is simpler than those models. Treat AU values as relative, within-person indicators. Note also that the best-supported frustration cues below (AU23/AU24) are among the hardest AUs to detect.

The face model returns 52 blendshape scores (brows, cheeks, eyes, jaw, mouth and nose), so many other movements are available. Their correspondence to FACS action units follows naming conventions, not validation, and the module currently uses 11 of them.

What the evidence suggests, in order

These are recommendations drawn from the evidence above, not results.

  1. Pupil from the eye tracker. The strongest physiological effort cue, and the suite already has the right sensor. Needs the tracker to export pupil diameter, plus luminance control.
  2. A separate fatigue indicator. Replace the fixed 500 ms eye-closure flag with PERCLOS-style closure fraction, blink duration and lid-reopening delay, kept out of the effort score. Validate the closure threshold, and check glasses and frame-rate limits.
  3. Lip tightener/presser as an added friction cue, after validation. It has the best direct evidence for frustration, but it is also hard to detect, so measure its accuracy against labelled data before it influences CFI.
  4. Use AU4 mainly for effort and concentration. The friction evidence for AU4 and AU14 alone is mixed, so friction should rest on co-occurring cues and task context.
  5. Leave out micro-expression detection, head movement as a load cue, and gaze aversion.

Fusion & insights

Gaze & AOI

  • Each face frame is matched to the nearest gaze sample within 250 ms; older gaze is ignored, so a stale AOI is never reported.
  • The active AOI is the rectangle containing the gaze point; if rectangles overlap, the one listed last (drawn on top) wins.
  • saccade_rate_hz counts samples flagged as saccades in the last 1 s.
  • RULA scores use the nearest sample within 2 s.

Compound posture risk

posture high = RULA neck ≥ 3 OR RULA trunk ≥ 3 risk = posture high AND ( AU7 ≥ 0.40 OR AU4 ≥ 0.50 )

A COMPOUND_POSTURE_RISK event is logged once when the condition starts, and again only after it has been clear for 1 s. A sustained condition therefore logs one event, not one per frame.

Correlated insight

One label per frame, chosen by the first rule that matches:

  1. FATIGUE_MICROSLEEP_RISK: eyes closed ≥ 500 ms
  2. AUTOMATION_SURPRISE: surprise flag is on
  3. POSTURE_DRIVEN_VISUAL_COMPENSATION: posture high and AU7 ≥ 0.40
  4. HIGH_COGNITIVE_STRAIN_ON_COMPLEX_WIDGET: CFI ≥ 0.70 while looking at an AOI
  5. HIGH_COGNITIVE_FRICTION: CFI ≥ 0.70, no AOI
  6. VISUAL_STRAIN_DISPLAY_LEGIBILITY: AU7 ≥ 0.40 with acceptable posture (suspect glare or low-contrast text, not posture)
  7. POSTURAL_LOAD_WITH_COGNITIVE_STRAIN: posture high and AU4 ≥ 0.50
  8. SUSTAINED_MENTAL_EFFORT: MES ≥ 60
  9. null: nothing notable

Timing & sync

  • Every frame is stamped at capture on one monotonic clock, so processing delay never becomes drift between streams.
  • UTC and LSL timestamps are derived by offsets that are re-checked every 10 s and slewed (at most 0.5 ms per check), never stepped, so time stays monotonic.
  • Each live payload carries clock_drift_ms, the current gap between derived UTC and the system clock, and processing_ms for the engine. Camera mode also reports inference_ms for the face tracker.
  • Inbound gaze, RULA and UI events may carry their own timestamp (t_utc_ms or t_lsl); they are matched by time, not arrival order.

What is and isn’t validated

  • Verified by tests: the maths above on synthetic faces (AU recovery across head pose, calibration, speech and blink handling, event timing) and that the browser engine matches the Python engine.
  • Not yet validated: accuracy against FACS-coded ground truth. The AU scales are hand-tuned. The PRD’s F1 ≥ 0.82 target on DISFA/BP4D is not yet met; the training tool (cogsense validate) exists, but those datasets are licensed and weren’t available.
  • MES and CFI weights (0.7/0.3, 0.6/0.4, 0.15) are design choices, not fitted to operator data (see research evidence for what the literature does and does not support). Treat absolute values as relative within a person and session until they are calibrated against ground truth such as NASA-TLX.
  • AU14 is weak from a monocular mesh; occlusion detection is heuristic (MediaPipe extrapolates hidden landmarks).

Tuning thresholds

Every constant on this page lives in CogSenseConfig. Override any of them with a JSON file:

{ "cfi_au4_threshold": 0.60, "mes_au4_weight": 0.8, "scales": { "brow_height": 0.07 } }

cogsense run --config overrides.json

The browser engine accepts the same keys: new CogSense.CogSenseEngine({ cfi_au4_threshold: 0.60 }).
